E9: ACQUISITION CHOICE AND THE COST OF A SAFE POLICY UPDATE
Local experiment completed 17 October 2026. No GitHub update or proposal edit.

QUESTION AND DESIGN
We tested three questions: whether conclusions depend on cost accounting;
whether an acquisition rule can choose trusted-only evaluation; and the smallest
tested cost at which a safe update meets a prespecified usefulness target.

The controlled population is the E8 four-context, two-action Bernoulli setting.
All candidates and their influence coefficients are fixed before evaluation.
Main and trusted data share the same population and randomized logger. Trusted
rewards are immutable and independent of the main sample. This is a synthetic
experiment, not a new result on OptDigits or an estimate of real acquisition costs.

The protocol was fixed before the full run. It includes 2,000 fresh independent
seed clusters, three signal strengths, both policy-influence geometries, nine
rules, six price ratios (1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50), two significance levels (0.01,
0.001), two attack models and two cost-accounting models. All conditions and
outcomes, including failures, are retained. The large number of decisions is
repeated evaluation on these clusters, not millions of independent replications.

COST AND THREAT ASSUMPTIONS
Total purchase: a main row costs 1 and a trusted row costs rho. Every method can
spend the same available budget on its own sample sizes, including buying more
cheap main rows. Main-sample availability is not artificially capped.
Historical sunk: a common 19,000-row main log is already available, and only new
trusted rows are charged. The all-main baseline has zero incremental cost.
These are different resource models and are analyzed separately.

The white-box attacker observes logs, candidate models, source weights and the
complete final rule. It changes only main rewards. One model permits up to 200
edits; the other permits up to 1% of main rows. The latter count changes with the
purchased sample size, according to the same declared threat rule for every
method. The attacker knows policy truth; the acquisition rule does not.

THE ACQUISITION RULE
For each feasible acquisition plan and source-weight pair, the rule calculates
an exact binomial null threshold h and the maximum count distortion d. It chooses
the smallest standardized attacked mean-approval barrier:
  (h - 0.5 + 2*d) / denominator - 0.5.
This uses only counts, costs, the declared attack and the allowed testing error.
Trusted-only and main-only are genuine acquisition endpoints: the rule can redirect
money into purchasing a different number of observations, rather than merely
ignoring data after paying for it. The rule is frozen before evaluation rewards.

The weight grid is expressed as integer per-row weights, rather than the E8 grid
of source fractions. It includes (main,trusted) weights (4,19), reproducing the E8
0.2 reference fraction at 19,000 main and 1,000 trusted rows. It also includes
(1,4), which the new criterion sometimes selects instead. E9 is a new controlled
comparison with fresh seeds and common uniform-null risk bounds. Its numbers
should not be compared as if they were the same E8 oracle risk-calibration run.

FINDING 1: COST ACCOUNTING CHANGES THE ANSWER
With a total budget of 20,000, price ratio 5, a 200-edit cap and low-influence good
policy, the rule buys 4,000 trusted observations and no main rows. Its improvement
is 0.267 percentage points (pp), compared with 0.180 pp for buying only main rows.
At price ratio 50 and the same budget, it buys only main rows, obtaining 0.180 pp;
trusted-only evaluation gives 0.020 pp. The rule can therefore choose either
endpoint without knowing which policy is good.

The stronger all-main comparator changes the interpretation of the older cost
example. At budget 69,000 and price ratio 50, buying 69,000 main observations
obtains 0.300 pp under a 200-edit cap. The fixed 19,000-main/1,000-trusted design
obtains 0.212 pp, and trusted-only obtains 0.101 pp. Buying more main rows dilutes
a fixed attack budget. The rate-based threat is reported separately; large logs
can also be useful there when the reward signal exceeds the corruption barrier.
It would be misleading to present expensive trusted data alone as a reason to mix.

FINDING 2: THE SELECTOR IS NOT AN EXPECTED-UTILITY OPTIMUM
At total budget 10,000 and price ratio 20 under a 200-edit cap, the selector chooses
main-only. Its gain is slightly negative and rounds to zero, whereas trusted-only
achieves 0.030 pp in the low-influence-good configuration and 0.274 pp after
reversing the influence relationship. Minimizing a mean-approval threshold can
misrank the finite-sample probability of a useful decision. Thus the acquisition
selector is a working baseline with a falsified universal superiority claim.
It should not be advertised as Pareto-optimal or as reliably choosing the best
allocation in every regime.

FINDING 3: A FIXED HISTORICAL LOG IS A BETTER MECHANISM EXAMPLE
With 19,000 existing main rows, a new-data budget of 10,000, price ratio 5 and a
200-edit cap, the budget buys 2,000 trusted rows. The source-weight rule gives
those rows weight 0.333. Results under the exact worst-utility attack are:

                                      Low-influence good     High-influence good
                                      gain pp / harm %       gain pp / harm %
Main only, no additional cost          0.137 / 0.200           1.234 / 0.150
Trusted only                          0.156 / 0.250           1.411 / 0.100
Equal pooling                         0.219 / 0.150           1.979 / 0.050
Influence-based error allocation       0.239 / 0.050           1.454 / 0.250
Independent pilot selection           0.163 / 0.000           1.466 / 0.000
Independent pilot error allocation    0.159 / 0.000           1.435 / 0.000
Source weighting / acquisition rule   0.257 / 0.200           2.319 / 0.000

All methods have a 1% analytic harmful-deployment ceiling, not identical actual
harm. Zero observed harm does not mean zero population risk. The weighted-minus-
influence contrast in the low-influence geometry is 0.018 pp, with pointwise
paired interval [0.013, 0.023]. Its conservative experiment-wide simultaneous
interval still includes zero. The reversal comparison is much stronger. Source
weighting and the new acquisition selector make identical decisions in this
example: the result supports evidence combination, not an additional gain from
acquisition optimization.

FINDING 4: USEFULNESS MUST BE MORE THAN SIMPLY POSITIVE
The primary usefulness target was 0.100 pp. In the historical-log setting above,
the main-only baseline already exceeds this target at zero additional cost.
Buying trusted data is not necessary for that modest target.

A second, prespecified target requires 75% of the best available policy gain.
This equals 0.225 pp in the low-influence geometry and 2.025 pp in the reversal.
At a new-data budget of 10,000, source weighting has simultaneous utility lower
bounds 0.244 and 2.209 pp, meeting both targets. Trusted-only evaluation fails at
this budget, with upper bounds 0.173 and 1.557 pp; it first meets both targets at
20,000 on the tested grid. This is a smallest-tested-budget comparison. The
experiment does not establish an exact twofold reduction in minimum population
cost; intermediate budgets were not tested.

STATISTICAL AND ATTACK CHECKS
Five correctness tests passed, including exhaustive small-sample attack search
and independent null calibration. The audit reconstructed all 44,064,000 clean
and attacked decisions, regenerated 234 source arrays, checked 14,528 design-grid
entries and 4,872 calibration cases, and verified feasible attack witnesses for
all evaluated seeds. Endpoint attacks attain the worst selected-policy utility
for this specific two-candidate monotone gate. No claim is made for arbitrary
learned-policy libraries. Runner calibration uses FFT convolution; the auditor
uses conditional binomial survival summation with an omitted-mass bound.

Utility intervals use exact Clopper-Pearson bounds on good/bad selection
probabilities with a Bonferroni correction across both conditions and all groups.
Their joint coverage is at least 95%. Paired bootstrap intervals are descriptive,
pointwise intervals. Harm guarantees are analytic uniform-null guarantees;
finite-sample harm frequencies may exceed a small nominal level. At alpha 0.001,
the largest observed frequency was 0.150%; that alone does not refute the bound.

Runtime: the simulation took 7.152 seconds, cached independent audit 3.175 seconds,
and analysis 11.282 seconds. An initial audit attempt was stopped because it
repeatedly decompressed the same arrays. audit_fast.py runs the frozen auditor
unchanged with arrays cached once; this changes I/O only. Implementation and
manual review time are not included in those timings. All three figures were
rendered and visually inspected.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR CHOOSING A PROJECT EXAMPLE
The fixed-historical-log, cost-versus-useful-improvement comparison is the strongest
candidate for presentation from this run. It has a clear resource constraint,
strong comparators, useful gain and a reversal test. The choice remains with the
user; no presentation, proposal or GitHub repository was updated.

The all-data-purchased result is an essential counterexample and should accompany
it. It prevents a claim that mixing is always worth paying for. The new selector
should be presented as exploratory until its threshold objective is replaced or
justified through a guarantee about decision power or expected utility. Real
acquisition costs, general learned policies, compromised references and source
shift remain unresolved. The current experiment tests assumed cost models; it
cannot establish that price ratio 5 or 50 describes a real deployment.
